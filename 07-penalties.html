Chapter 7

What Happens If He Doesn't

Non-willful and willful penalties, reasonable cause, and the procedures that exist for people who are late but not crooked

[ILLUSTRATION -- a calendar with April 15 circled, a small worried Hipster looking at it from a distance]

By the time Hipster understood what he owed, the penalty section is what actually got him to sit down and file. It's worth reading carefully, because the range between "forgot" and "hid it on purpose" is enormous, and the law treats those two things very differently.

A non-willful violation, missing the filing or getting it wrong without intent, carries a civil penalty of up to $10,000 per violation under the original statute, a figure adjusted annually for inflation under the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act. As of the most recent adjustment reflected in 31 CFR § 1010.821 (effective January 17, 2025), that adjusted maximum is $16,536 per violation. There is a real exception built into the statute itself: no penalty applies if the violation was due to reasonable cause and the account balance was otherwise properly reported on the taxpayer's income tax return.

A willful violation, filing that's deliberately false or a deliberate failure to file, is a different order of magnitude. Under 31 U.S.C. § 5321(a)(5)(C), the civil penalty is the greater of $100,000 (also inflation-adjusted, currently $165,353 per 31 CFR § 1010.821) or 50 percent of the balance in the account at the time of the violation. On a large account, the 50 percent figure can dwarf the flat-dollar number, which is exactly what makes willfulness the line that matters most.

Beyond civil exposure, 31 U.S.C. § 5322 creates criminal penalties for willful violations: a fine of up to $250,000, imprisonment of up to five years, or both, under § 5322(a). If the violation occurs while breaking another federal law, or as part of a pattern of illegal activity involving more than $100,000 in a 12-month period, § 5322(b) raises that to a fine of up to $500,000, imprisonment of up to ten years, or both.

None of that is Hipster's situation. He's late, not hiding anything, with nothing more sinister than not knowing the rule existed. For someone in that position, the IRS maintains the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures: file the delinquent FBARs, attach a statement explaining the late filing, and if the income was already properly reported and taxed, no penalty is typically imposed. For someone who also has unreported foreign income to fix, the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures address both the returns and the FBARs together, with reduced or no penalty depending on residency.

The Law, Actually

  • 31 U.S.C. § 5321(a)(5)(B) -- non-willful civil penalty, statutory cap of $10,000 per violation, inflation-adjusted to $16,536 per violation under 31 CFR § 1010.821 (as of the Jan. 17, 2025 adjustment).
  • 31 U.S.C. § 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii) -- reasonable cause exception: no penalty if the failure was due to reasonable cause and the account was properly reported on the income tax return.
  • 31 U.S.C. § 5321(a)(5)(C) -- willful civil penalty, the greater of $100,000 (inflation-adjusted to $165,353 under 31 CFR § 1010.821) or 50 percent of the account balance at the time of the violation.
  • 31 U.S.C. § 5322(a) -- criminal penalty for willful violations: fine up to $250,000, imprisonment up to 5 years, or both.
  • 31 U.S.C. § 5322(b) -- enhanced criminal penalty when combined with another federal law violation or a pattern exceeding $100,000 in illegal activity within 12 months: fine up to $500,000, imprisonment up to 10 years, or both.
  • IRS Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures; Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures -- administrative relief paths for late, non-willful filers, described on irs.gov.

Bottom Line

Penalty amounts adjust for inflation, so treat the specific dollar figures above as current as of this chapter's last check, not fixed forever. The line that matters most is willful versus non-willful; for someone who is simply late and has nothing to hide, the delinquent-filing procedures exist precisely so that filing late beats not filing at all.
