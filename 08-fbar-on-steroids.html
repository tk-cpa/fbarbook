Chapter 8

FBAR on Steroids

Form 8938, the second foreign-asset form -- higher thresholds abroad, a wider list of assets, and why filing one never replaces the other

[ILLUSTRATION -- two forms side by side on a desk, one labeled FinCEN and one labeled IRS, Hipster between them with a calculator]

A week after Hipster filed, the same friend who'd asked about the FBAR sent a follow-up: "Great. Did you do the 8938 too?" Hipster stared at the number for a while. It was not a typo this time. It was a second form.

Form 8938 asks a similar question in a different place, to a different agency, with different rules. It's attached to the income tax return rather than filed separately, it goes to the IRS rather than FinCEN, and it's due when the return is due, extensions included. It comes from 26 U.S.C. § 6038D, which requires an individual holding an interest in a specified foreign financial asset to report it if the total value passes a threshold.

The threshold is where it parts ways with the FBAR. There's no flat $10,000 line. The trigger depends on where the filer lives and whether the return is joint. For a US-based unmarried filer it's more than $50,000 on the last day of the year or more than $75,000 at any time. For a filer who qualifies as living abroad, which means a bona fide resident of a foreign country for a full tax year or present abroad at least 330 full days in a 12-month period, the numbers jump to more than $200,000 at year-end or more than $300,000 at any time. Hipster has been abroad for four years, and the account totals he pulled for the FBAR were nowhere near $200,000. On those facts, Form 8938 probably isn't his problem. If he moved back to the US, the lower thresholds would apply and the answer could change.

The two forms also disagree about what counts. For the FBAR, signature authority alone is enough, which is why the company account from Chapter 3 is on his list. For Form 8938, a foreign account where the filer has only signature authority is not reportable on that basis alone. But Form 8938 reaches things the FBAR doesn't: under § 6038D(b), a specified foreign financial asset includes not just accounts at foreign financial institutions but also stock or securities issued by a non-US person and any interest in a foreign entity. Hipster's 20 percent stake in the company is not an FBAR item. It is exactly the kind of asset Form 8938 counts toward its threshold.

Joint accounts work differently too. A married couple filing jointly files one combined Form 8938. Hipster isn't married to his girlfriend, so for a joint account with her the instructions have him report the maximum value of the entire account, regardless of how much of it is his.

The penalties are separate and they stack. A failure to file Form 8938 carries a $10,000 penalty, with up to $50,000 more if the failure continues after the IRS notifies the filer, for a potential total of $60,000. Reasonable cause, not willful neglect, is a defense. And filing Form 8938 never replaces the FBAR: the instructions say so in plain terms.

The Law, Actually

  • 26 U.S.C. § 6038D(a), (b) -- an individual holding an interest in a specified foreign financial asset must attach the required information to the return if the aggregate value exceeds the threshold; the term covers financial accounts at foreign financial institutions, non-US stock and securities, investment contracts with a non-US counterparty, and interests in foreign entities.
  • 26 CFR § 1.6038D-2(a) -- the reporting thresholds: $50,000 / $75,000, $100,000 / $150,000 for joint filers, and $200,000 / $300,000 and $400,000 / $600,000 for qualifying residents abroad.
  • Instructions for Form 8938 -- definition of a specified individual and of a qualified individual abroad (bona fide residence for a full tax year, or 330 full days in 12 months); one combined form for spouses filing jointly; the maximum value of an entire jointly held account is reported by a non-spouse co-owner; reasonable cause defense; filing Form 8938 does not relieve the filer of the FBAR.
  • IRS, Comparison of Form 8938 and FBAR Requirements (page last reviewed September 19, 2026) -- thresholds, signature-authority treatment, and filing and due-date mechanics side by side.
  • 26 U.S.C. § 6038D(d) -- penalty for failure to furnish the information: $10,000, with an additional $10,000 for each 30-day period after the IRS notice period, capped at $50,000 of additional penalty.

Bottom Line

Two forms, two agencies, two sets of rules. The FBAR has the low trigger and catches signature authority; Form 8938 has the high trigger abroad and catches foreign company interests. Hipster likely owes the FBAR and not Form 8938, but that depends on staying a qualifying resident abroad and on the total value of everything Form 8938 counts, including his stake in the company. Thresholds are set by regulation, so confirm the current figures for the year being filed.
