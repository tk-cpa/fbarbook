Chapter 2

The Account With His Own Name On It

The plain-vanilla case: a personal foreign bank account over $10,000

[ILLUSTRATION -- a local bank branch storefront, Hipster walking out holding a debit card and a confused expression]

The first account is the simplest one, which is exactly why Hipster almost missed it. Not long after landing, he walked into a local bank, showed his passport, and opened a checking account so he could get paid, pay rent, and stop losing money to wire fees. Over the year, freelance income came in, rent went out, and the balance bounced between the equivalent of $2,000 and $14,000 depending on the week.

He never thought of it as anything but a checking account. But under 31 CFR § 1010.350(b), a "financial interest" exists whenever a US person is the owner of record or holder of legal title to the account, regardless of whether the account earns income, regardless of whether any money ever comes back to the US, and regardless of whether he's ever filed a US return reporting activity from that country. Ownership alone is enough.

The number that matters is not his balance on December 31. It's the highest combined value his foreign accounts touched at any single point during the year, converted to US dollars using the Treasury's year-end exchange rate for FBAR purposes. Hipster's account alone hit $14,000 mid-year on a single invoice payment before rent cleared a few days later. That one moment is what puts him over the $10,000 aggregate threshold, even though his balance on most days was much lower.

"Foreign financial account" also reaches further than a checking account. The regulation's definition in 31 CFR § 1010.350(c) covers bank accounts, securities and brokerage accounts, mutual fund-type accounts, and certain insurance or annuity policies with cash value, if the financial institution maintaining them is located outside the United States. A local checking account is the easy case. A local brokerage or retirement account he opens later would count the same way.

The Law, Actually

  • 31 CFR § 1010.350(b) -- financial interest includes being the owner of record or holder of legal title, regardless of whether the account produces income.
  • 31 CFR § 1010.350(c) -- defines "foreign financial account" to include bank, securities, and other financial accounts maintained by a financial institution located outside the United States.
  • FinCEN Form 114 Instructions -- the $10,000 threshold is measured on the highest aggregate value across all foreign accounts at any point in the calendar year, not the year-end balance, converted using the Treasury's published year-end exchange rate.

Bottom Line

Owning a foreign personal account is the most straightforward FBAR trigger there is. There's no minimum balance that has to be sitting there on any particular date; a single high point in the year is enough, and every foreign account gets added together to test the $10,000 line.
