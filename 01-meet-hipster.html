Chapter 1

Meet Hipster

Why an American with a foreign bank account has a filing obligation nobody warned him about

[ILLUSTRATION -- Hipster staring at a "FinCEN Form 114" search result on his laptop, three empty espresso cups next to him]

Hipster left the US four years ago with a backpack, a laptop, and a plan that changed twice before he'd finished unpacking. He opened a local bank account because nobody takes American checks. He started a small company because the visa rules made that the easiest way to stay. He fell for someone who was not American, and eventually they opened an account together to split rent. None of it felt like a tax event. It felt like Tuesday.

Then a friend, also American, also abroad, sent him a message: "Did you file your FBAR yet?" Hipster had never heard the word. He assumed it was a typo for FUBAR, which, once he read the penalty section, felt uncomfortably close.

Here is the part almost nobody tells Americans before they move: the United States taxes its citizens and residents on their worldwide income no matter where they live, and separately from that, it wants to know where their money physically sits. That second requirement is the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts, universally shortened to FBAR, filed as FinCEN Form 114. It is not a tax form. It reports no income and creates no tax due on its own. It is a disclosure, filed with the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, not the IRS, even though the IRS enforces it.

The obligation is old. It comes from the Bank Secrecy Act, specifically 31 U.S.C. § 5314, which lets the Treasury Secretary require US persons to keep records and file reports when they have a relationship with a foreign financial agency. The operative regulation, 31 CFR § 1010.350, is where the actual rule lives: a US person with a financial interest in, or signature authority over, one or more foreign financial accounts must file an FBAR if the aggregate value of those accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the calendar year.

Three words in that sentence do most of the work, and the rest of this book is really just unpacking them one scenario at a time: aggregate (every foreign account gets added together, not tested account by account), at any point (a single high-balance day counts, even if the account was empty by December 31), and signature authority (which, as Hipster is about to learn, does not require owning a cent of the money).

The Law, Actually

  • 31 U.S.C. § 5314 -- statutory authority requiring US persons to report foreign financial accounts.
  • 31 CFR § 1010.350 -- defines who must file: a US person with a financial interest in, or signature or other authority over, foreign financial accounts with an aggregate value over $10,000 at any time during the calendar year.
  • FinCEN Form 114 (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts) Instructions -- the form itself; filed electronically through the BSA E-Filing System, not attached to a tax return.

Bottom Line

If you are a US citizen or resident (or a domestic entity) and your combined foreign accounts ever crossed $10,000 during the year, even for a day, you likely have an FBAR to file. It is separate from your income tax return and has its own filing system. The next chapters walk through Hipster's specific accounts, because "foreign account" turns out to cover more than he thought.
