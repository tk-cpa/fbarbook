Chapter 3

The Company Account He Can Move Money From

Signature authority over his own foreign company's account -- and why that's a separate trigger from ownership

[ILLUSTRATION -- Hipster at a desk, laptop open to an online banking portal, company logo on a coffee mug]

To keep his visa and get paid properly, Hipster set up a small local company. He's the majority owner and the only person who can log into the company's bank account and move money -- pay a vendor, cover a subscription, send himself a distribution. The company account never held more than the equivalent of $30,000, and he never thought of that money as "his" the way he thought of his personal checking account. It was the business's money.

That distinction matters for the company's own filings, but it doesn't get Hipster out of an FBAR. There are two separate reasons his name attaches to that account, and either one alone would be enough.

The first is signature authority, defined at 31 CFR § 1010.350(f)(1) as the authority to control the disposition of money in the account by direct communication, in writing or otherwise, to the bank. If Hipster can wire money out or sign a payment order, he has signature authority over that account, full stop, regardless of whether a dollar of it belongs to him personally.

The second is financial interest through ownership, defined at 31 CFR § 1010.350(e)(2), which reaches a US person who directly or indirectly owns more than 50 percent of the voting power, total value of equity interest, or profits interest of an entity that owns the account. As majority owner, Hipster has a financial interest in the company's account even setting signature authority aside entirely.

There is a narrow signature-authority-only exemption in 31 CFR § 1010.350(f)(2) for certain officers and employees of banks, SEC-registered investment advisers, and similarly regulated entities, when they have signature authority but no financial interest of their own and the entity itself files its own FBAR. That exemption exists for people like a corporate treasurer at a large regulated company, not for the majority owner of a small foreign business. It doesn't touch Hipster's case at all.

The Law, Actually

  • 31 CFR § 1010.350(f)(1) -- signature authority is the authority to control disposition of account assets by direct communication to the institution holding the account.
  • 31 CFR § 1010.350(e)(2) -- financial interest through an entity includes owning, directly or indirectly, more than 50 percent of the entity's voting power, equity value, or profits interest.
  • 31 CFR § 1010.350(f)(2) -- a narrow exemption for signature authority alone, limited to officers/employees of specified regulated entities with no financial interest of their own; does not apply to majority owners.

Bottom Line

Being able to move money out of an account is its own FBAR trigger, separate from owning the money. Hipster's company account is reportable twice over: he has signature authority over it and, as majority owner, a financial interest in it too. The account gets reported on his personal FBAR, and it's aggregated with his personal account and anything else foreign he holds when testing the $10,000 line.
