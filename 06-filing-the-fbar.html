Chapter 6

Actually Filing the Thing

FinCEN Form 114, the BSA E-Filing System, deadlines, and the automatic extension nobody has to ask for

[ILLUSTRATION -- Hipster's laptop screen showing the BSA E-Filing System homepage, a checklist of account numbers taped to the monitor]

Once Hipster accepted that he had three accounts to report, personal, company, and joint, the mechanics turned out to be less dramatic than the research getting there. The FBAR is not attached to a tax return, and it is not filed through the IRS's e-file system. It's filed directly with FinCEN through the BSA E-Filing System, a free government portal, and it can be filed by the individual or by a paid preparer with power of attorney on file.

For each foreign account, the form asks for the same core facts: the name of the financial institution, its address, the account number, the type of account, the maximum value during the year, and whether the filer's interest is ownership, joint ownership, or signature authority only. Hipster needed his year-end and mid-year statements from all three accounts to pin down each account's maximum value accurately, which took longer than the filing itself.

The due date is April 15 of the year following the calendar year being reported, the same date his 1040 is due, a deliberate alignment created by the Surface Transportation and Veterans Health Care Choice Improvement Act of 2015, which directed Treasury to move the FBAR deadline to match. What surprised Hipster was the extension: unlike almost every other filing he'd dealt with, he did not have to request anything. FinCEN grants an automatic extension to October 15 to any filer who misses April 15, with no form, no letter, and no reason required. If he'd owed nothing and just filed late within that window, he'd have been on time.

One filing does not answer everything, though. The FBAR reports the existence and value of foreign accounts. It says nothing about whether income earned in those accounts, interest, dividends, gains, was properly reported on his 1040, and it says nothing about whether his ownership of a foreign company triggers a separate information return of its own. Those are different questions, covered elsewhere in this series.

The Law, Actually

  • 31 CFR § 1010.306(c) -- sets the FBAR due date at April 15 following the calendar year reported.
  • Surface Transportation and Veterans Health Care Choice Improvement Act of 2015, Pub. L. 114-41, § 2006(b)(11) -- statutory directive requiring Treasury to align the FBAR due date with the individual income tax filing deadline.
  • FinCEN guidance implementing the 2015 Act -- grants an automatic extension to October 15 for any filer who misses April 15, with no extension request required.
  • FinCEN Form 114 Instructions -- filed electronically through the BSA E-Filing System; not filed with, or attached to, the federal income tax return.

Bottom Line

File FinCEN Form 114 through the BSA E-Filing System, separately from the tax return. The deadline is April 15, but missing it isn't fatal by itself: the automatic extension to October 15 applies to everyone without a request. Filing the FBAR on time is not the same as having correctly reported foreign income or foreign entity ownership on the return itself.
